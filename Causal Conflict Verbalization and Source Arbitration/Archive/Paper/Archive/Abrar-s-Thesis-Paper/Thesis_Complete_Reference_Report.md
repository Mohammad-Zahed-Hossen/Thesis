## 2. One-Paragraph Summary (executive abstract, plain but precise)

The thesis asks whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can be used to automatically generate realistic, structurally valid **adversarial (manipulative/coercive) versions of real Bangla telephone conversations**, and whether transformer-based text classifiers can then be trained to detect the difference between genuine and adversarially-rewritten conversations. The author collected ~50 hours of real Bangla phone calls from an education consultancy (customer service, admissions inquiries, IELTS/study-abroad counseling, follow-ups, promotional calls), transcribed them with Google Cloud Chirp V3 (Whisper/WhisperX was tried and rejected due to hallucination/looping), manually role-annotated 7.5 hours (agent="a", customer="c"), then fine-tuned Gemini 2.5 Flash on that 7.5-hour gold set to auto-annotate the remaining 42.5 hours. Using a hand-built **"conversational coercion and persuasion" taxonomy** (financial pressure, false guarantees, urgency framing, emotional coercion, document hostage, bait-and-switch, etc.), the author prompted two different LLMs — **Gemini 2.5 Flash** (cloud) and **Qwen 2.5 14B** (local, open-source) — to rewrite the agent's lines into manipulative "predatory twins" while keeping the customer's original lines untouched and preserving conversational structure. After automated + manual cleaning to remove hallucinated artifacts, the final dataset was used to fine-tune five multilingual/Bangla transformer encoders (**BanglaBERT, XLM-RoBERTa, DistilBERT-multilingual, IndicBERT, MuRIL**) as binary classifiers (genuine=0 vs. adversarial=1), using a sliding-window tokenization strategy (512-token windows, 256-token stride) to handle long conversations. Models were evaluated in two **cross-domain** setups: (Experiment 1) train on Genuine+Gemini, test on unseen Qwen-generated data; (Experiment 2) train on Genuine+Qwen, test on unseen Gemini-generated data. Results: in-domain-style performance (Exp 1) was strong across all models (~95.7%–96.9% accuracy), but cross-domain performance (Exp 2) collapsed for several models (IndicBERT down to 79.6% accuracy, DistilBERT to 82.1%), while BanglaBERT and XLM-RoBERTa stayed robust (94.5% and 96.7% respectively). The core finding: **adversarial text generated by different LLMs has distinct, generator-specific stylistic "fingerprints" (token length, verbosity, phrasing), so classifiers tend to learn generator-specific artifacts rather than a truly generalized notion of "manipulation," and adversarial data from different LLMs cannot be treated as one homogeneous class.** The thesis contributes (1) a new real-world Bangla telephonic dataset, (2) a taxonomy-driven adversarial generation framework/pipeline, and (3) an empirical vulnerability map of five transformer architectures under LLM-generated adversarial stress.

## 3. Research Problem, Questions, and Objectives (verbatim-accurate)

### 3.1 Background motivation
- LLMs can generate synthetic data at scale, but **low-resource languages (especially Bangla)** are underrepresented in training corpora and adversarial-evaluation benchmarks.
- Real Bangla phone speech has code-switching ("Banglish"), colloquialism, and pragmatic complexity that rule-based/deterministic systems can't replicate.
- Bangladeshi educational/administrative call centers (admissions counseling, IELTS coaching sales, promotional outreach) are a rich, high-volume, realistic source of this kind of data.
- Adversarial training is known to improve transformer robustness (citing Goodfellow et al. and Jia & Liang), but manually building high-quality adversarial *conversational* data is expensive and slow — hence the motivation to automate it with LLMs.

### 3.2 Problem statement (two root gaps)
1. **Data Scarcity and Pipeline Isolation** — no curated, domain-specific Bangla conversational datasets from real telephonic environments exist; models train on "clean" academic data that fails in production.
2. **Absence of Adversarial Benchmarks** — no standardized framework/taxonomy exists for generating structural, localized adversarial data in Bangla, so deployed models' defensive limits are unknown.

### 3.3 Research Question (verbatim)
> "Is it possible to produce structurally consistent and linguistically valid adversarial Bangla conversational data using real-world phone call transcripts while avoiding any semantic drift or data leakage issues caused by large language models?"

### 3.4 Objectives
1. Generate cleaned and structured Bangla conversation data from real phone call recordings.
2. Assess several transcription and annotation pipelines for low-resource speech data.
3. Create adversarial versions with both local and cloud-based LLMs.
4. Evaluate language drift and structural maintenance in generated adversarial data.
5. Train and evaluate several transformer models on real vs. adversarial datasets.
6. Determine cross-domain robustness of different models trained on different adversarial sources.

### 3.5 Stated Contributions
1. **Real-World Dataset** — a new dataset of structured, cleaned Bangla telephonic educational/admin counseling interactions.
2. **Adversarial Generation Framework** — a taxonomy-driven architecture using both a commercial cloud API (Gemini) and a local open-source LLM (Qwen) to generate semantically-bounded adversarial variants.
3. **Empirical Vulnerability Mapping** — benchmarking of state-of-the-art transformers (e.g., BanglaBERT) showing specific performance-degradation thresholds under synthetic adversarial evaluation.

## 4. Literature Review — Key Cited Works and Their Role

The thesis situates itself against four literature threads. (Reference numbers below match the thesis's own numbered bibliography [1]-[19].)

### 4.1 Transformer architectures & language modeling
- **BERT** (Devlin et al. [6]) — bidirectional MLM+NSP pretraining; GLUE 80.5%; limited by 512-token input cap and heavy compute cost.
- **DistilBERT** (Sanh et al. [7]) — knowledge distillation; 40% smaller (66M vs 110M params), ~97% of BERT's performance, faster inference, but *more vulnerable to adversarial/OOD noise*.

### 4.2 Multilingual & low-resource language modeling
- **XLM-RoBERTa** (Conneau et al. [8]) — 2.5TB Common Crawl, 100 languages, 250K vocab; beat mBERT on XNLI by +14.6%; still weak on very low-resource, morphologically rich languages.
- **IndicBERT** (Kakwani et al. [9]) — trained on IndicCorp (11 Indic languages, 3.4B tokens), ~11M params (very compact); weak on dialectal/colloquial/informal text.
- **MuRIL** (Khanuja et al. [10]) — 16 Indian languages + transliteration pairs; +10% over mBERT on regional NER/QA; limited generalization outside its Indian-language benchmark set.
- **BanglaBERT** (Sarker et al. [11]; refined by Bhattacharjee et al. [3]) — RoBERTa-style model trained on 27.5GB / 2.4B-token Bangla web corpus with Bangla-specific tokenization; macro F1 = 93.4% on Bangla sentiment tasks. This is the paper's flagship "language-specific pretraining wins" example.

### 4.3 Adversarial vulnerability & text perturbation
- **TextFooler** (Jin et al. [12]) — black-box word-substitution attack; dropped BERT accuracy from 95.6% to <10% on AG News/IMDB while keeping 0.91 semantic similarity; needs domain-specific synonym dictionaries.
- **BERT-Attack** (Li et al. [13]) — uses BERT's own masked-LM head to generate replacement tokens; >95% attack success, 25% fewer substitutions than TextFooler (more fluent), but computationally expensive.
- **ANLI** (Nie et al. [14]) — human-and-model-in-the-loop adversarial NLI dataset built over 3 rounds; exposed that lexical-only models fail on pragmatic structure; risk of unnatural/biased generated samples.

### 4.4 Synthetic data generation & long-sequence handling
- **Self-Instruct** (Wang et al. [15]) — bootstrapped 52,000+ instructions from 175 seed tasks; +33% absolute on Super-NaturalInstructions; risk of amplifying model bias.
- **LLM-synthesis survey** (Ding et al. [16]) — taxonomy of label-free / label-constrained / sequence-mapping synthetic generation pipelines.
- **"The Curse of Recursion" / Model Collapse** (Shumailov et al. [17]) — training on model-generated data recursively causes irreversible degradation (probability-distribution tails vanish). This directly motivates the thesis's emphasis on cleaning/validating synthetic data rather than blindly trusting it.
- **Longformer** (Beltagy et al. [18]) — sliding-window + global attention, scales to 4096 tokens, BPC=1.10 on text8; weak for short-text classification.
- **Big Bird** (Zaheer et al. [19]) — sparse (random+local+global) attention, O(N) complexity; strong on long-doc QA/retrieval but can lose fine-grained turn-level pragmatic detail — cited as the reason the thesis prefers a practical sliding-window tokenization approach instead of adopting BigBird/Longformer directly.

### 4.5 The Three Research Gaps the Thesis Claims to Fill

| Gap | Description |
|---|---|
| **Gap 1 — Monolingual Bias in Adversarial NLP** | Benchmarks like GLUE/ANLI/TextFooler are English-only; English perturbation strategies don't translate to Bangla due to typographic/morphological/syntactic differences. |
| **Gap 2 — Lack of Connection with Real-Life Data** | Prior robustness work uses formal document-level text (Wikipedia, news); ignores multi-turn spoken conversation constraints (speaker roles, turn-taking, colloquial code-switching). |
| **Gap 3 — Uncontrolled Semantic Drift in Synthetic Generation** | No existing pipeline has automated, validated constraints to prevent semantic drift/context dropping/data contamination during LLM-driven conversational data synthesis. |

The thesis's stated response: **"a taxonomy-driven role-preserving generation framework explicitly for low-resource Bangla telephonic datasets, while providing empirical performance degradation baselines for downstream transformer architectures."**

## 5. Methodology — Full Pipeline (6 Phases)

The overall architecture (Figure 3.1) has six sequential phases:

```
[1] Raw Data Ingestion & Processing
        ↓
[2] Human Core Annotation (7.5 hrs, manual, gold standard)
        ↓
[3] Gemini Fine-Tuning (on the 7.5-hr gold set)
        ↓
[4] Automated Annotation Expansion (42.5 hrs auto-labeled → 50 hrs total corpus)
        ↓
[5] Synthetic Corpus & Adversarial Generation (taxonomy-driven, Gemini + Qwen)
        ↓
[6] Model Training & Evaluation (5 transformer encoders, cross-domain testing)
```

### 5.1 Phase 1 — Data Collection
- Source: real phone calls at **Penston Academy** (an education consultancy).
- Call types: customer service, admission/course inquiries, follow-up communication, promotional/feedback calls.
- Recording devices: **Tecno KL5** and **Tecno Spark 30C**, using the phones' built-in call recorder.

### 5.2 Phase 1b — Audio Preprocessing
- Converted AAC/MP3 → WAV.
- Standardized to **16 kHz sampling rate**.
- Applied high-pass and low-pass filters to normalize call quality.
- Discarded duplicate files.
- Kept only recordings **longer than 30 seconds**.
- Renamed files using timestamp metadata.

### 5.3 Phase 1c — Transcription (ASR) and the Chirp vs. Whisper Decision
Two ASR systems were tested:
- **Google Cloud Chirp V3** — large-scale encoder-decoder architecture. **SELECTED.** Produced stable, phonetically accurate, grammatically valid transcriptions even with low-bitrate cellular background noise.
- **Whisper / WhisperX** (including fine-tuned variants with forced timestamp alignment) — **REJECTED.** Suffered "localized attention-map collapse" on noisy telephonic audio: it would correctly transcribe the opening, then fall into a catastrophic hallucination loop, repeating the same phrase (e.g., an institution's name) at near-uniform timestamp intervals for the rest of the segment. A documented example (audio file `20251007_163128.wav`) showed 5+ consecutive "Duplication Iteration" segments where Whisper just repeated "Pentson Academy" verbatim instead of transcribing new speech.

This is a **key empirical/engineering finding**, not just a methods footnote: it demonstrates a real ASR failure mode (attention collapse / hallucinatory looping) on noisy low-resource telephonic audio.

### 5.4 Phase 2 — Manual (Human) Annotation
- Role tags: **`a`** = agent, **`c`** = customer.
- **7.5 hours** of audio were manually role-annotated by the author — this becomes the "gold standard" ground truth.
- Sensitive information was normalized/redacted for privacy compliance.

### 5.5 Phase 3 — Gemini Fine-Tuning for Automated Annotation
- To avoid a manual-annotation bottleneck for the full 50 hours, the 7.5-hour gold set was used to **fine-tune Gemini 2.5 Flash via Vertex AI** to learn the dialogue's structural/role patterns.
- Training used platform-default hyperparameters (no manual tuning), run for **4 epochs**.
- **Loss:** started ~5.3 (Epoch 1) → dropped to ~0.5 (Epoch 4), monotonic decrease.
- **Token-level training accuracy:** started ~0.30 (Epoch 1) → rose to ~0.91 (Epoch 4), steady increase, no sign of overfitting to the small baseline sample.

### 5.6 Phase 4 — Automated Annotation Expansion + Structural Validation
- The fine-tuned Gemini model auto-annotated the remaining **42.5 hours** of unlabeled genuine dialogue → total corpus scaled to **50 hours**.
- To validate that the auto-labeling didn't introduce structural noise (hallucinated turns, speaker-role collapse, unnatural lengths), two macro-structural metrics were computed and compared between the human-gold subset and the machine-annotated subset:
  1. **Speaker Proportion** — the density/presence ratio of each speaker role in a conversation.
  2. **Turn Alternation Rate** — how often speaker roles switch, as a proxy for natural dialogue flow.
- **Result:** negligible variance between human and machine annotation — **~0.003 difference in speaker proportion** and **~0.003 difference in turn alternation rate** — confirming the auto-annotation preserved authentic human turn-taking structure rather than introducing generator artifacts.

### 5.7 Phase 5 — Adversarial Generation

#### 5.7.1 The Coercion/Persuasion Taxonomy
A custom taxonomy of manipulation tactics observed in real educational-consultancy calls was built to guide/constrain the LLM rewriting. Full list as given in the thesis (Coercion Taxonomy + Appendix A):
- Persuading for guaranteed success in fixed days/months (IELTS)
- Claiming IELTS score guarantees university scholarships
- Discrediting IDP/British Council to push specific affiliations
- Promoting illegal proxy exams for hefty sums
- Pressuring for study-abroad files immediately after IELTS results
- Pestering uninterested customers
- Claiming Spoken English certificates are broadly versatile for jobs
- Promoting irrelevant services (Kids/Professional English) mid-call
- Pushing affiliated universities solely for commission
- Giving false hope despite poor academic/financial profiles
- Forging bank documents for illegal financial support
- Charging for visa applications with "guaranteed" refunds
- Giving illegal spouse-visa application advice
- Asking for money for IELTS registration/date changes
- Fake work-permit/company-coalition scams (Europe-focused)
- Selling local "foundation courses" as fake "abroad" solutions
- Document Hostage (retaining passport/certificates)
- Hidden Costs (undisclosed accommodation/service fees)
- Exploiting "No-IELTS" routes with high rejection risk
- Psychological manipulation (parental pressure, guilt)
- Black-market VFS (visa facilitation service) appointment claims
- Post-visa university swapping (bait-and-switch)

#### 5.7.2 Models Used for Generation
- **Gemini 2.5 Flash (API/cloud)** — PRIMARY generator. Chosen for strong instruction-following, structural consistency, multilingual (Bangla) support. Produced the initial adversarial rewrite of each agent line, conditioned on the coercion-aware system prompt.
- **Qwen 2.5 14B (local, open-source)** — SECONDARY generator. Took Gemini's cleaned output as context and performed further paraphrasing/diversification while preserving structure and speaker alignment.

#### 5.7.3 Models Rejected for Generation
| Model | Reason for rejection |
|---|---|
| Phi-4 (14B, local) | Inconsistent adherence to dialogue structure; frequent loss of speaker-role integrity |
| LLaMA-4 Maverick 17B (Maas API) | High output-formatting variance; semantic drift beyond acceptable thresholds |
| DeepSeek R1 (local) | Instability under constrained prompting; unreliable instruction-following |

#### 5.7.4 Generation Constraints (from the actual system prompt used, Appendix B)
The Gemini system instruction cast the model as a **"Senior Linguistic Researcher specializing in Predatory Discourse Analysis"** with these rules:
- Preserve exact chronological turn order of the input.
- Keep every original customer (`c`) line **completely unchanged**.
- For every agent (`a`) line, generate an adversarial "predatory twin" (`a_adv`) plus a tactic label (`a_tactic`) from the predatory taxonomy, with a brief rationale for why that tactic fits the context.
- **Greeting Protection:** Islamic greetings (আসসালামু আলাইকুম / ওয়ালাইকুম আসসালাম) must remain unmodified — no coercion may be attached to greetings.
- **Deceptive Politeness:** must keep the formal "আপনি" (Apni) pronoun — the manipulative agent should still sound outwardly professional, like a real rogue salesperson, not cartoonishly villainous.
- **Style Match:** must mirror the exact register of informal Bengali / slang / English code-switching present in the original transcript.
- Output format enforces strict tagging (e.g., input `c, a, c` → output `c, a_adv, a_tactic, c`).

### 5.8 Phase 5b — Adversarial Cleaning
Because LLMs hallucinate and occasionally ignore instructions, post-generation cleaning removed:
- Non-Bangla content (via language-drift detection)
- Leftover artifact/helper text (e.g., stray tactic tags, meta-commentary)
- One-line or incomplete dialogues
- Conversations with fewer than two complete agent-customer turns
- Noise such as repeated greetings/affirmations/filler

**Documented example of the full cleaning lineage** (thesis Section 3.8.1), traced through one identical conversational window:
1. **Raw Gemini output** — contains bracketed tags like `[a_tactic] File Pressure`, `[a_tactic] Local Foundation Trap`, and diagnostic annotations mixed into the dialogue text.
2. **Cleaned Gemini output** — all procedural tags/brackets scrubbed; leaves a clean role-annotated turn sequence.
3. **Raw Qwen output** — takes the cleaned Gemini text as context, performs its own paraphrasing (compressing/altering lines), but injects its own timestamp index arrays (e.g., `[0009]`, `[0010]`, `[0011]`).
4. **Cleaned Qwen output** — timestamp headers stripped, final purified role-annotated text ready for model training/evaluation.

### 5.9 Phase 5c — Final Dataset Shape (Table 3.3, exact numbers)

| Metric | Exp.1 Train (Gemini) | Exp.1 Val | Exp.1 Test | Exp.2 Train (Qwen) | Exp.2 Val | Exp.2 Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total document records | 3,075 | 543 | 604 | 2,987 | 528 | 604 |
| Total cleaned token count | 793,073 | 133,560 | 120,331 | 581,482 | 99,180 | 155,338 |
| Average token length | 257.9 | 246.0 | 199.2 | 194.7 | 187.8 | 257.2 |
| Length variance scale (I_f) | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

- A **stratified 70/15/15 split** was used for train/validation, preserving class balance.
- Labels: **genuine = 0, adversarial = 1** (binary classification).
- Test sets were drawn from a **disjoint adversarial source** to force true cross-domain evaluation (i.e., the classifier never sees the "test" generator's style during training).

### 5.10 Phase 6 — Model Training Setup
- **Sliding-window tokenization**: each conversation tokenized without truncation, then split into overlapping chunks of **512 tokens with a stride of 256 tokens** (i.e., 50% overlap) to respect transformer input limits without discarding context. Each chunk inherits the parent conversation's label.
- **Baseline**: logistic regression on vectorized (TF-IDF-style) text, used as a lightweight reference point.
- **Primary models (5 transformer encoders)**, each fine-tuned for binary classification using a classification head over the `[CLS]` token:
  1. **BanglaBERT**
  2. **XLM-RoBERTa**
  3. **DistilBERT** (multilingual variant)
  4. **IndicBERT**
  5. **MuRIL**
- **Training hyperparameters**: 3 epochs, **AdamW** optimizer, batch size 32, learning rate 2×10⁻⁵.
- **Evaluation-time aggregation**: chunk-level predictions are combined via **mean probability pooling** to produce one final conversation-level prediction (since a long conversation may span multiple 512-token chunks).

### 5.11 The Two Cross-Domain Experiments (core evaluation design)
- **Experiment 1**: Train on (Genuine + Gemini-adversarial), **Test on unseen Qwen-adversarial**.
- **Experiment 2**: Train on (Genuine + Qwen-adversarial), **Test on unseen Gemini-adversarial**.

This design directly tests **generator-transfer robustness**: can a detector trained on one AI's manipulative "style" catch a different AI's manipulative style it has never seen?

## 6. Results — Exact Numbers and Interpretation

### 6.1 Evaluation Metrics Used
- **Accuracy** = (TP+TN) / (TP+TN+FP+FN)
- **Precision** = TP / (TP+FP); **Recall** = TP / (TP+FN)
- **Macro-F1** = unweighted mean of F1 for genuine class and adversarial class (chosen specifically to be insensitive to class-size imbalance)
- **ROC-AUC** = area under True-Positive-Rate vs. False-Positive-Rate curve across all thresholds

### 6.2 Overall Performance Table (Table 4.1, exact figures)

**Interpretation:**
- In **Experiment 1** (trained on Gemini adversarial data, tested on unseen Qwen data), **all five models perform strongly and similarly** (95.7%–96.9% accuracy) — Qwen's outputs, being short and stylistically uniform, seem to be "easy" to generalize to once a model has learned from the richer/more diverse Gemini data.
- In **Experiment 2** (trained on Qwen adversarial data, tested on unseen Gemini data), there is a **severe, uneven collapse**: XLM-RoBERTa (96.7%) and BanglaBERT (94.5%) stay strong, but **IndicBERT crashes to 79.6%**, **DistilBERT to 82.1%**, and MuRIL drops moderately to 89.2%.
- **Overall conclusion:** XLM-RoBERTa is the most cross-domain-robust model; BanglaBERT is the best "language-specialist" performer; IndicBERT and DistilBERT are the most fragile under this specific direction of domain shift (Qwen→Gemini).

### 6.3 Confusion Matrix Deep-Dive (Section 4.3)

**Best-performing configurations (clean diagonal confusion matrices):**
- **BanglaBERT (Exp.1)**: AUC = 0.9872. Of 302 true genuine sequences: 7 False Positives, 14 False Negatives. Interpreted as: monolingual Bangla pretraining gives it tight, language-specific syntactic anchors that catch Qwen-style perturbations even without having seen Qwen data.
- **XLM-RoBERTa (Exp.2)**: AUC = 0.9809. Misclassified only 3/302 genuine conversations (True Negative = 299/302). 17 False Negatives on Gemini's adversarial style. Its massive multilingual embedding space bridges the style gap between Qwen (train) and Gemini (test).
- **DistilBERT (Exp.1)**: AUC = 0.9772. 286 true adversarial vectors caught, only 4 false alarms — shows parameter distillation preserves core attention mechanisms for this (easier) transfer direction.

**Worst-performing / "domain collapse" configurations:**
- **IndicBERT (Exp.2)**: Correctly identified genuine samples ([0][0] = 293) but suffered **114 False Negatives** — i.e., it failed to flag **37.7%** of Gemini's adversarial conversations, essentially defaulting to predicting "genuine" for almost everything. It could not transfer its learned Qwen-perturbation patterns to Gemini's very different patterns.
- **DistilBERT (Exp.2)**: Complete collapse in the reverse direction — **105 False Negatives**, while still keeping a very high True Negative count (299/302). This asymmetry shows Gemini's more sophisticated adversarial style was mistaken for genuine, authentic customer speech by the lightweight distilled model.
- **MuRIL (Exp.2)**: Weakest overall in this group — AUC = 0.9275, **53 False Negatives** and the **highest False Positive count** among the compared group (12). Its broad multi-Indic-language focus apparently blurs the fine pragmatic cues that separate authentic Bangla dialogue from Gemini-crafted manipulation.

**Two key empirical takeaways stated explicitly in the thesis (Section 4.3.3):**
1. **Leakage Mitigation Validity** — False Positives stayed low and stable across ALL six configurations (3–12 range), which the author interprets as proof that the genuine test samples were properly isolated from training (i.e., there is no data leakage inflating the results — the models are learning real distinguishing signal, not memorizing).
2. **Asymmetric Cross-LLM Generalization** — the central fragility is a steep False-Negative spike specifically in Experiment 2 (Qwen→Gemini direction, up to 114 misses), while the reverse direction (Gemini→Qwen, Experiment 1) stays robust. The author concludes **Gemini 2.5 Flash generates more linguistically diverse, complex, unconstrained adversarial patterns**, making it the superior *training* source for building generalizable defenses, while Qwen's narrower, more uniform outputs make for a weaker training source (a model trained only on Qwen doesn't "see enough variety" to catch Gemini's tricks).

### 6.4 Token Distribution Analysis (Section 4.4, Table 4.2)

| Domain | Count | Min | Max | Mean | Median | Std. Dev |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Genuine | 2,013 | 35 | 2,613 | 270.985 | 204 | 221.172 |
| Gemini | 1,907 | 30 | 2,170 | 347.039 | 292 | 213.268 |
| Qwen | 1,804 | 11 | 306 | 206.562 | 225 | 48.236 |

**Distributional "fingerprints" (via KDE plot, Figure 4.3):**
- **Genuine**: broad, natural distribution, peaks early (~100–150 tokens) — short, spontaneous, asymmetric turns typical of real human speech.
- **Gemini**: extensively right-skewed tail extending past 1,000 tokens — tends to "over-explain," generating long, detailed, prolonged multi-turn outputs.
- **Qwen**: sharply concentrated (leptokurtic) spike strictly between ~150–300 tokens — very rigid/uniform output length, unlike either genuine speech or Gemini's variability.

**Downstream implications identified by the author:**
- **Asymmetric Chunking Density** — Gemini's long tail means it produces disproportionately more 512-token sliding-window chunks during training, over-weighting the model's exposure to Gemini's specific vocabulary/style.
- **Uniformity Exploitation risk** — Qwen's narrow, uniform length distribution could let a classifier "cheat" by learning length as a shortcut/proxy for the Qwen-adversarial label, rather than learning genuine semantic/pragmatic manipulation cues.
- **Linguistic Length Bias risk** — because genuine human data skews toward shorter sequences than either LLM's output, models risk learning a spurious "long = adversarial" heuristic, which would misfire on legitimately long genuine conversations.
- Longer-sequence training data (Gemini) → risk of **overfitting to repetition patterns**; shorter-sequence training data (Qwen) → risk of **underfitting complex conversational structure**.

### 6.5 Adversarial Source Separability Analysis (Section 4.5, Table 4.3)

A separate experiment: can a simple **logistic regression on TF-IDF features** tell Gemini-generated adversarial text apart from Qwen-generated adversarial text (i.e., are the two LLMs' "manipulative styles" themselves distinguishable, independent of the genuine-vs-adversarial task)?

| Setting | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 | AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gemini vs Qwen (in-domain) | 0.937 | 0.898 | 0.981 | 0.938 | 0.984 |
| Train on Gemini → Test on Qwen | 0.931 | 0.886 | 0.998 | 0.939 | 0.981 |
| Train on Qwen → Test on Gemini | 0.890 | 0.825 | 0.997 | 0.903 | 0.960 |

**Conclusion drawn:** Even a simple linear TF-IDF classifier can separate the two LLMs' adversarial outputs with very high accuracy (~89–94%). This proves that **"adversarial-ness" generated by an LLM is not a single unified concept — it is strongly generator-dependent**, with each LLM leaving a distinguishable lexical/stylistic signature. The asymmetry (Qwen→Gemini transfer is notably worse than Gemini→Qwen transfer) mirrors the same directional weakness found in the main transformer classification experiments (Section 6.3 above).

### 6.6 Summary of Findings (as stated by the author, Section 4.6)
1. Transformer models can effectively distinguish genuine vs. adversarial conversations under controlled (in-domain) conditions.
2. Cross-domain generalization remains a significant challenge due to generator-specific stylistic differences.
3. Sliding-window tokenization enables handling of long conversations but introduces chunk-level biases (over-representation of longer sources).
4. Balanced evaluation is critical to avoid misleading performance metrics.
5. Mixed adversarial training improves robustness but does not fully eliminate domain dependency.
6. **Core thesis claim:** *Adversarial Bangla conversational data is generator-dependent and cannot be treated as a homogeneous class without explicit domain-invariant training strategies.*

---

## 7. Conclusion (Chapter 5, condensed but complete)

- Language-specific pretraining matters: **BanglaBERT** (trained specifically on Bangla) reached elite AUC (0.9872) under adversarial stress, outperforming broader cross-lingual models in that particular configuration — supporting the value of Bangla-specific tokenization/vocabulary over generic multilingual sub-word approaches.
- Cross-domain training exposed a **profound stylistic asymmetry** between Gemini and Qwen as adversarial-data generators: models trained on Qwen data are fragile against Gemini's outputs (up to 114 False Negatives), while models trained on Gemini's more diverse outputs are robust against Qwen's narrower outputs.
- The overarching claim: this research **bridges the gap between clean academic NLP benchmarks and fragile real-world (production) environments** by proving synthetic adversarial datasets can be engineered — via a taxonomy-driven, role-preserving, multi-LLM pipeline — to expose real model vulnerabilities, without uncontrolled semantic drift or data leakage. This is offered as a foundation for building more secure, robust Bangla conversational NLP systems.

---

## 8. Limitations (Chapter 5.2, as stated by the author)

1. **ASR Fidelity Dependency** — the whole pipeline depends on Google Chirp V3's transcription accuracy; acoustic noise or phonetic ambiguity in raw audio propagates downstream into role-labeling and generation errors.
2. **Structural Instability / Language Drift in Generation** — high-temperature sampling and few-shot prompting (in both cloud and local LLMs) frequently caused semantic drift, syntactic inconsistency, or non-native phrasing, requiring aggressive post-generation filtering.
3. **Sequence-Length / Context Constraints** — the fixed 512-token transformer window forces sliding-window chunking, which (even with optimized stride) limits models' ability to see full-length, discourse-level adversarial signals spanning an entire phone call.
4. **Domain Specificity / Lexical Bias** — the corpus comes exclusively from Bangladeshi educational-agency interactions, so classification boundaries may reflect domain-specific vocabulary/context rather than universal "manipulation" patterns that would generalize to other domains (e.g., banking, medical).
5. **Stochastic Variation in Generation Quality** — the marked stylistic/length divergence between Gemini and Qwen outputs creates a distribution mismatch that directly undermines cross-domain generalization (this is the same phenomenon documented empirically in Sections 6.2–6.5 above).

---

## 9. Future Work (Chapter 5.3, as proposed by the author)

1. **Domain-Specific Foundation Models** — pretrain/fine-tune native open-source Bangla LLMs specifically for spontaneous, idiomatic dialogue, reducing dependence on proprietary APIs and synthetic language drift.
2. **End-to-End Speech-Aware Pipelines** — move away from separating ASR and text perturbation; explore injecting adversarial perturbations directly at the acoustic/audio-feature level, or use ASR-error-robust encoders, to reduce error propagation from transcription mistakes.
3. **Hierarchical / Sparse Attention Architectures** — replace the sliding-window constraint with long-context architectures (Hierarchical Transformers, BigBird, Longformer) to capture full long-range, multi-turn conversational context.
4. **Contrastive / Self-Supervised Learning** — use contrastive objectives (e.g., InfoNCE loss) to force the encoder to represent genuine vs. adversarial "twins" based on underlying intent/semantics rather than surface-level generator-specific stylistic tokens — directly targeting the generator-fingerprinting problem found in Sections 6.3/6.5.
5. **Multi-Domain Corpus Expansion** — extend beyond educational consulting to other conversational domains (medical helplines, banking customer service, e-commerce telecom) to build broader cross-domain robustness.

## 10. Full Terminology / Abbreviation Glossary

| Term | Meaning (as used in this thesis) |
|---|---|
| **Adversarial data / adversarial example** | Text deliberately perturbed/manipulated to test or expose weaknesses of a model, or here, text rewritten to contain manipulative/coercive sales tactics |
| **Adversarial Bangla conversation** | A version of a real conversation where the agent's lines have been rewritten by an LLM to include coercive/persuasive tactics, while the customer's lines remain unchanged |
| **Genuine class (label 0)** | The original, real (unmodified) human conversation |
| **Adversarial class (label 1)** | The LLM-manipulated version of a conversation |
| **ASR** | Automatic Speech Recognition — converting audio to text (here: Google Chirp V3 vs. Whisper/WhisperX) |
| **Attention collapse / hallucinatory looping** | A failure mode (observed in WhisperX) where the model gets "stuck" repeating the same output instead of transcribing new audio |
| **Cross-domain evaluation** | Testing a model on data generated by a *different* source/generator than the one it was trained on (here: trained on one LLM's adversarial style, tested on another's) |
| **Fine-tuning** | Further training a pretrained model (here: Gemini 2.5 Flash) on a small labeled dataset (7.5 hrs of gold annotation) to specialize it for a task (auto-annotation) |
| **Sliding-window tokenization** | Splitting a long text into overlapping fixed-size chunks (512 tokens, 256-token stride) so it fits transformer input limits without losing context at chunk boundaries |
| **[CLS] token classification head** | Standard BERT-family method: a special token's final hidden representation is fed into a classifier layer to make a prediction for the whole input |
| **Mean probability pooling** | Averaging the predicted probabilities across all chunks of one conversation to get one final conversation-level prediction |
| **Macro-F1** | F1 score averaged equally across classes, regardless of how many examples are in each class (protects against misleading results from class imbalance) |
| **ROC-AUC** | A threshold-independent measure of how well a classifier separates two classes across all possible decision cutoffs |
| **Speaker Proportion** | A metric measuring how much of a conversation (by presence) belongs to each speaker role |
| **Turn Alternation Rate** | A metric measuring how often the conversation switches between speakers — a proxy for natural conversational flow |
| **Taxonomy-driven generation** | Generating adversarial text guided by a predefined, explicit list of manipulation/coercion tactic categories, rather than open-ended prompting |
| **Predatory twin** | The author's term (used in the actual Gemini system prompt) for the manipulative, coerced rewrite of an originally neutral agent utterance |
| **Model Collapse / "Curse of Recursion"** | The phenomenon (Shumailov et al.) where training models recursively on model-generated data causes irreversible quality degradation — cited as a risk the cleaning pipeline aims to avoid |
| **Code-switching / "Banglish"** | Mixing Bangla and English within the same sentence/conversation, common in real Bangladeshi speech |
| **Data leakage** | When information from the test set inadvertently "leaks" into training, inflating apparent performance — the thesis explicitly checks for and reports on this (see Section 6.3, "Leakage Mitigation Validity") |

**Standard NLP/ML abbreviations used:** AI (Artificial Intelligence), ANLI (Adversarial Natural Language Inference), API (Application Programming Interface), BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), BPC (Bits Per Character), CSE (Computer Science and Engineering), GLUE (General Language Understanding Evaluation), GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer), LLM (Large Language Model), LM (Language Model), MLM (Masked Language Modeling), MuRIL (Multilingual Representations for Indian Languages), NER (Named Entity Recognition), NLI (Natural Language Inference), NLP (Natural Language Processing), NLU (Natural Language Understanding), NSP (Next Sentence Prediction), RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Approach), XLM-R (Cross-lingual Language Model - RoBERTa), XNLI (Cross-lingual Natural Language Inference).
